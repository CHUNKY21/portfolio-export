Meridian Exception Manager

From a flat queue to a ranked worklist the team trusts

An AI layer that detects, prioritizes, and proposes remediation for operational exceptions — trade breaks, mismatches, failed reconciliations — presenting them as a ranked, actionable worklist instead of a flat queue. The push/ambient counterpart to the Meridian Research Agent's pull-only design.

IntroductionMeridian Exception Manager

What's Meridian Exception Manager?

A system that continuously watches for operational exceptions and surfaces them as a ranked worklist with a visible reason for every flag and a proposed next step — instead of a flat queue an analyst has to triage from scratch. Anonymized composite of real BlackRock work; Anchorpoint Capital and Meridian are fictional stand-ins for the actual client/platform, since that work is current-employer confidential.

The problems

  1. Exceptions arrived as a flat queue with no reliable signal for what actually mattered
  2. Prioritization depended on tribal knowledge that lived only in experienced analysts' heads
  3. High-risk exceptions could sit unworked for hours with nothing distinguishing them from routine ones

Business Goals

  • Faster exception resolution, fewer missed high-risk breaks
  • Fewer aged/unresolved exceptions
  • Analysts trust the system's prioritization enough to act on it

UX Strategy

  • Contextual interviews to capture experienced staff's implicit rules
  • Visible reasoning for every flag, by default
  • Low-friction dismissal path that visibly feeds back into the system
  • Aging made visible, not silent

Feature Set

  • Prioritized worklist (ranked, not flat)
  • Proposed-remediation card
  • Workflow states — AI-proposed vs. human-confirmed
  • Aging/escalation indicator + dismissal feedback loop
Design & ProcessMeridian Exception Manager

The 8-Stage AI-Enhanced UX Process

1. Discovery & Research

Same: contextual interviews with the manual-triage team and their team lead.

New: mapped what the system could rank with confidence (severity, exposure, recurrence) vs. what needed human judgment (regulatory sensitivity, client context) — and, because this is push/ambient, mapped the analyst's trust baseline toward being told something needs attention vs. being answered on request.

2. Problem Definition / Pain Points

Same: synthesized manual-triage pain points into a clear problem statement.

New: separated "the queue is unranked" (fixable by the system) from "I don't trust being interrupted by an automated flag" (fixable only by interaction design).

3. Competitive / Pattern Analysis

New: benchmarked proactive alerting tools (fraud/risk alerting, IT ops incident triage) specifically on how they handle being wrong when pushing something at a user, not just feature parity. Alerts with no visible reason trained users to ignore them within weeks — that became the design bar here.

4. Design Thinking / Ideation

New: ideation centered on the system's behavior at the moment it interrupts someone — what it shows when not highly confident, what happens after a dismissal, where "detect and propose" ends and "human must confirm" begins.

5. Design Sprints & Iteration

Same: iterative sprints, weekly usability testing.

New: the earliest version ranked with a bare numeric score; testing showed analysts distrusted it as "another black box," so iteration replaced the score with a plain-language reason shown inline before any click.

6. Usability Testing

New: tested whether analysts worked the list top-to-bottom as ranked (trust) or re-sorted it themselves (distrust signal), and deliberately tested the over-flagging failure path, not just the happy path.

7. Delivered Solution

Same: shipped v1 with a clear before/after.

New: continuous detection and ranking, a visible reason and proposed remediation on every item, explicit AI-proposed vs. human-confirmed workflow states, first-class dismissal path feeding back into ranking.

8. Outcomes / Metrics

See Results below — illustrative targets, since no sourced measured figure exists for this concept in the project's files.

Unexpected Challenge

The riskiest early design choice wasn't the ranking algorithm — it was showing a bare priority score with no reason. Testing showed a system pushing an unexplained number is trusted less than a flat, unranked queue, because it reads as a black box deciding for you rather than a tool helping you decide.

What We Learned

Replacing the score with a visible, plain-language reason shown directly on the worklist row — before any click — was the single highest-leverage change in the whole iteration cycle.

Key InsightMeridian Exception Manager
This is the deliberate push/ambient contrast to Epic 1's pull-only research agent — same platform, same underlying trust-building principle (never surface a conclusion without a visible reason), applied to a completely different interaction mode. Epic 1 has to earn trust when asked a question; Epic 2 has to earn trust while interrupting someone who didn't ask for anything. That's why alert fatigue and false-positive distrust, not accuracy alone, were the core design challenge.
